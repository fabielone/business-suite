# Team task queues and claiming: principles, claim concurrency, offline conflicts, permissions

Scope: principles and patterns for an offline-first (browser, Android/iOS, Windows/macOS) small-business app where a task routes to a person, a team, or an unclaimed team queue that members claim. The server is authoritative; offline authorization is last-known only. Research run 2026-11-03. Sources marked "(not fetched this session)" are well-known primary references cited from prior knowledge; verify wording before quoting them.

## 1. Pull systems, single accountable owner, diffusion of responsibility, cherry-picking

### Takeaway
Kanban gives the rationale for a shared queue that members pull from when they have capacity, with WIP limits and an explicit commitment point. Product practice (Asana) and RACI agree that each item needs exactly one accountable owner. Experimental evidence (Barron and Yechiam 2002) shows group-addressed requests get fewer and worse responses than requests addressed to one person, which argues for turning "team-owned" into "person-owned" quickly. Field evidence from radiology and emergency medicine shows that when people choose their own work they favour quick or easy items, and in radiology that discretion measurably reduced productivity. Queue policy (ordering, age visibility, escalation) therefore matters as much as the claim mechanism.

### Cited Findings
**Pull and WIP (Kanban Guide; practitioner standard, not empirical)**
- Kanban calls signals of available capacity "pull signals". Work is drawn in "only if there is capacity", and "completed work is regarded as more valuable than starting new work." — [Kanban Guide, Kanban University](https://kanban.university/kanban-guide/)
- A WIP limit is "the maximum number of work items allowed at a time". It can apply to a state, person, lane, work type or whole system, and serves as "an enabling constraint" that encourages focus and collaboration. — [Kanban Guide](https://kanban.university/kanban-guide/)
- Every Kanban system has "at least one clear commitment and delivery point", and lead time starts at commitment. Replenishment policies (when, how much, by whom) should be made explicit. — [Kanban Guide](https://kanban.university/kanban-guide/)

**Single accountable owner (product practice and convention, not empirical)**
- Asana deliberately allows one assignee per task so responsibility is never ambiguous. Its recommended pattern is one assignee on the parent task, with subtasks assigned to individuals. — [Asana, "Why one assignee?"](https://asana.com/resources/why-one-assignee); [Asana blog](https://blog.asana.com/?p=3269)
- Asana also documents assigning copies of a task to several people, which is fan-out, as the alternative to multi-assignee. — [Asana, assign copies of a task](https://asana.com/id/inside-asana/assign-copies-of-a-task.md)
- Users push back persistently (for example, wanting 40 people on one task), so demand for shared tasks is real. — [Asana forum thread](https://forum.asana.com/t/assign-multiple-assignees-on-one-task/1176?page=6)
- RACI convention: exactly one Accountable per task, while Responsible may be shared. Widely repeated, but I found no primary PMI text stating it, only secondary pages (for example [Cornell IT RACI/RASCI definitions](https://it.cornell.edu/index.php/it-service-management/raci-and-rasci-definitions) and [Asana RACI chart guide](https://asana.com:443/resources/raci-chart)). Treat any attribution to PMI as unverified.

**Diffusion of responsibility (experimental, empirical)**
- Barron and Yechiam (2002), *Computers in Human Behavior* 18:507–520, ran a field experiment with an email request for information. Requests sent to one recipient drew more responses, and those responses were more helpful and longer than replies to requests sent to several recipients. The authors attribute this to diffusion of responsibility: each recipient assumes someone else will answer. — [Paper record](https://datalearner.com:8443/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/72/paper-detail/71520)
- Blair, Thompson and Wuensch (2005) found that the virtual presence of many others significantly reduced email helping, though nonresponse did not rise in proportion to group size. — [Blair, Thompson and Wuensch 2005 (PDF)](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Blair-Thompson-Wuensch-2005.pdf)

**Cherry-picking under self-selection (empirical field data)**
- Radiology, "Discretionary Task Ordering: Queue Management in Radiological Services" (Ibanez, Clark, Huckman and Staats; SSRN 2017, later published in *Management Science*). The UNC page lists neither authors nor journal; both come from prior knowledge. Across 2.4 million diagnoses, doctors deviated from the managed order to batch similar tasks and to pick tasks they expected to finish faster. Using random assignment to queues, the authors found these deviations reduced productivity, and prioritising the shortest tasks was "particularly detrimental". Self-chosen batching also hurt, unlike batching imposed by managers. Discretion increased with experience, while the productivity penalty shrank. — [UNC CBOH abstract](https://cboh.unc.edu/publication/discretionary-task-ordering-queue-management-in-radiological-services/); [Kenan Institute listing](https://kenaninstitute.unc.edu/?p=3218)
- Emergency department: Patterson et al. (2016), *Academic Emergency Medicine*, reportedly found that residents signed up sooner for patients with simpler chief complaints, in one year of data at a site with more than 50,000 visits. The authors could not tell preference for simple cases from aversion to complex ones. This comes from search summaries; I did not read the primary text. A related poster is Innes (2018), "Do doctors cherry-pick?" — [CJEM abstract MP40](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-emergency-medicine/article/mp40-do-doctors-cherry-pick/2ABBCBEE077D69CAA4939C34863FCC97)
- An experimental preprint on job ordering, autonomy and queue visibility reports that cherry-picking is associated with errors, but that the association is driven by selection rather than causation. It is a preprint and I did not verify its details. — [arXiv 2607.28820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28820)
- Dispatch versus choice (ride-hailing). A project write-up argues that auto-accept centralised matching reduces idle search and rider wait compared with driver-accept. This is not peer reviewed. — [Centralized matching on ride-hailing platforms](https://miaowei.netlify.app/Research/publications/centralized_matching/main.html). Qualitative work finds workers value choice, even when it is narrow — [synapsesocial record](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W4392855331). A CSCW 2025 paper finds newcomers follow recommendations while experienced workers deviate from them — [CSCW 2025 PDF](https://shunanjiang.github.io/files/cscw_2025_MR.pdf).

### Inferences
- Model three routing states explicitly: assigned to a person (one accountable owner), assigned to a team and claimed (team plus person), and assigned to a team and unclaimed (the team queue). "Unclaimed team" is a transient state, not an ownership state. Show its age, and give it an escalation owner, such as the team lead, so it is never "everyone's and nobody's". This follows from the Barron and Yechiam and Asana/RACI findings.
- Self-selection invites cherry-picking. To counter it: default sort by priority or age rather than ease; show how long each item has waited; optionally offer "take next" (system picks) alongside free choice; and let leads see aging unclaimed items. This follows from the radiology and ED evidence. The radiology result is about productivity loss, so in a small-business setting the main risk is starved hard or unpleasant tasks rather than throughput.
- A per-person WIP limit can be enforced as a soft warning at claim time, for example "you already hold N active tasks". Small teams may not want hard limits. Hard versus soft is a product decision.

### Gaps
- I found no primary PMI source for "one A". It is a convention.
- I found no empirical study on cherry-picking in small-business or field-service team queues specifically. The evidence comes from healthcare and gig platforms.
- I did not obtain primary text for the Patterson ED study.

## 2. Concurrency of claims: first claim wins

### Takeaway
"First claim wins" is best done as a single atomic conditional write on the server: claim only if the task is still unclaimed (or still at the expected version), and report how many rows changed. `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` is the right tool for "give me the next available item" dispatch, not for a person claiming a specific item. Leases with expiry, in the style of SQS visibility timeouts, suit machine workers. For human claims they should be at most an optional nudge, because silently expiring a person's claim breaks accountability.

### Cited Findings
- PostgreSQL docs: "With `SKIP LOCKED`, any selected rows that cannot be immediately locked are skipped. Skipping locked rows provides an inconsistent view of the data, so this is not suitable for general purpose work, but can be used to avoid lock contention with multiple consumers accessing a queue-like table." `NOWAIT` errors instead of waiting. — [PostgreSQL SELECT, Locking Clause](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-select.html)
- Under READ COMMITTED, an `UPDATE` that finds its target row changed by a concurrent committed transaction re-evaluates its `WHERE` clause against the updated row. As a result, `UPDATE task SET assignee=$me WHERE id=$id AND assignee IS NULL` affects 0 rows for the loser. — [PostgreSQL Transaction Isolation](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/transaction-iso.html) (not fetched this session)
- HTTP optimistic concurrency: `If-Match` with an entity tag makes a state-changing request conditional, and the server responds `412 Precondition Failed` if the representation changed. — [RFC 9110 §13.1.1 If-Match](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#name-if-match) (not fetched this session)
- Leases in SQS: a received message becomes invisible to other consumers for the visibility timeout. If it is not deleted before expiry, it "becomes visible again in the queue and can be retrieved by another consumer". The lease can be extended with `ChangeMessageVisibility` or a heartbeat, and released early by setting the timeout to 0. Because delivery is at-least-once, duplicates are still possible, so handlers must be idempotent. — [AWS SQS visibility timeout](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/sqs-visibility-timeout.html)

### Inferences
- **Conditional update (recommended for human claims).** Use `UPDATE ... SET assignee_id=$me, claimed_at=now(), version=version+1 WHERE id=$id AND business_id=$biz AND assignee_id IS NULL AND team_id = ANY($my_teams) RETURNING *`. Zero rows means the claim lost, or the caller is not entitled. Re-read the task to tell which, and return a typed outcome such as `already_claimed_by` or `not_permitted`. This is one round trip, needs no explicit locks, and works with idempotency keys.
- **Version or ETag compare-and-set.** Use this for general edits to a task, such as reassigning or unclaiming, where the client must prove it saw the current state. It is stricter than `assignee IS NULL`. Fields unrelated to the claim, such as an edited note, would also cause a rejection, so scope the precondition to the assignment fields where possible.
- **SKIP LOCKED.** Use this for "take next" or auto-dispatch (`SELECT id ... WHERE team_id=$t AND assignee_id IS NULL ORDER BY priority, created_at FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1`, then `UPDATE`), and for background job workers. It does not help an offline client, because the lock lives only inside a server transaction.
- **Leases.** For humans, prefer "claimed N days ago with no activity: lead is notified" over auto-release. Auto-release is a silent state change to an accountable record. If auto-release is ever used, it must be an explicit, audited, server-side event visible to the former claimant.
- **Idempotency.** Every queued offline command (claim, unclaim, complete) should carry a client-generated command ID so that retries after flaky sync do not double-apply. This follows the at-least-once reasoning from the SQS docs.

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative comparison benchmark between these patterns for low-contention small-team workloads. They are likely equivalent in cost there. The choice is about semantics, not performance.

## 3. Offline-first conflict handling for assignment fields

### Takeaway
The mainstream sync engines are server-authoritative for writes. PowerSync, ElectricSQL and Rocicorp Zero/Replicache all route client changes through your backend, which may reject them, after which the optimistic local state is replaced by server truth. Last-writer-wins is the common default for plain fields, and Linear reportedly finds it sufficient. LWW is wrong for claims, because the second offline claimant would silently steal the task. CRDT approaches (Ink & Switch) preserve concurrent values for the application to resolve, but by the essay's own account they cannot enforce access control. So a claim made offline should be a pending intent ("claim requested"), confirmed or rejected by the server, and a rejection must be shown to the user, never silently overwritten.

### Cited Findings
- **PowerSync.** The default is "essentially last write wins" per field, customisable on your backend. A rejected write is not rolled back explicitly: the winning server state syncs down and overwrites the local change. For conflicts or validation errors, do not return 4xx, because it blocks the upload queue. Return 5xx only for temporary errors, which are retried. For conflicts that cannot be resolved automatically, either persist them to a server-side dead-letter or conflict table and acknowledge, or "save both versions in a write_conflicts table and sync that back to the client so the user can decide." — [PowerSync: handling update conflicts](https://docs.powersync.com/handling-writes/handling-update-conflicts); [custom conflict resolution](https://docs.powersync.com/handling-writes/custom-conflict-resolution); [handling write validation errors](https://docs.powersync.com/handling-writes/handling-write-validation-errors); [writing client changes](https://docs.powersync.com/handling-writes/writing-client-changes)
- **ElectricSQL.** "Electric does not do write-path sync"; writes go through your API. The guide describes four patterns: online writes; ephemeral optimistic state; shared persistent optimistic state; and through-the-database sync with PGlite and a change log. Rejection strategies range from clearing all local state, to "marking writes as rejected for manual resolution", to clearing only causally dependent writes. With API writes the write context is still available at rollback time. With through-the-database sync, "the original write context is much harder to reconstruct". The guide also says conflicts are "extremely rare" in practice. — [Electric writes guide](https://electric.ax/docs/guides/writes)
- **Rocicorp Zero.** Server mutators are authoritative and "the server can add extra checks to enforce permissions". If a server mutator throws, "the entire mutation is rolled back", `handleMutateRequest` skips it and returns a structured error, and "the optimistic mutation on the client will be reverted". Clients can await `.server` to see the outcome. Identity comes from `ctx` (session), not from arguments, which users "cannot control". — [Zero mutators docs](https://zero.rocicorp.dev/docs/mutators); [Zero custom mutators](https://zero.rocicorp.dev/docs/custom-mutators). Edge cases have existed, for example an unregistered mutator retried indefinitely — [Rocicorp bug 3045](https://bugs.rocicorp.dev/issue/3045)
- **Linear.** A summary of Tuomas Artman's talk says Linear's sync engine uses an in-memory normalised object graph and that LWW "is enough for their needs". This is a secondary source, not Linear documentation. — [Fujimon, Linear's sync engine architecture](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://www.fujimon.com/blog/linear-sync-engine). A DEV article claiming Linear uses CRDTs conflicts with this and looks unreliable — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/johalputt/internals-how-linear-16-syncs-issues-across-100-engineer-teams-faster-than-jira-103-4hki)
- **CouchDB/PouchDB.** CouchDB "picks one arbitrary revision as the 'winner', using a deterministic algorithm". Losing revisions are kept and can be listed with `?conflicts=true` (`_conflicts`). The application must resolve them by writing a merged revision and deleting the others. Views see only the winner, so information can seem to disappear until the conflict is resolved. — [CouchDB replication and conflicts](https://docs.couchdb.org/en/stable/replication/conflicts.html)
- **Ink & Switch, "Local-first software" (Kleppmann et al., 2019).** Conflicts arise "only if users concurrently modify the same property of the same object". For those, "the CRDT keeps track of the conflicting values, and leaves it to be resolved by the application or the user". The authors also note that access control concepts "do not directly apply in a local-first context" and that "any user who has a copy of some data cannot be prevented from locally modifying it". By contrast, in cloud apps the server copy "is treated as the primary, authoritative copy". — [Ink & Switch essay](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/)
- **General LWW warning.** LWW "can silently discard meaningful edits". When a server rejects a write, the client should fetch the latest state and version token and avoid blind retry loops. This is a secondary source. — [Cursa, conflict detection patterns](https://cursa.app/en/page/conflict-detection-and-resolution-patterns)

### Inferences
- Treat claim, unclaim, reassign, complete and acknowledge as **commands** (intent plus expected precondition), not as field writes. The local store shows "Claim pending" (distinct styling, not "Mine") until the server confirms it. This matches the project's rule to keep pending intent separate from confirmed records.
- The server decides with a conditional update (§2) under the *current* membership and permissions, not those the client last knew. A rejection becomes a durable, synced **outcome record**, for example "Your claim of 'Call back Ana' at 10:42 offline was not applied: Marta claimed it at 10:31." Do not just let the server state overwrite the local state, which is PowerSync's default behaviour. Shown this way, the user learns that their work is not theirs. The pattern draws on PowerSync's write_conflicts table and Electric's "mark as rejected".
- Do not use LWW or CRDT registers for the assignee. LWW lets the last offline claimant steal the task. A multi-value register would leave two simultaneous "owners", which violates the one-accountable rule. CRDTs and LWW remain fine for collaborative free-text such as notes.
- Work done offline after a claim later rejected (notes, photos, time logged) must not be discarded. Keep it attached to the task under the user who did it, and flag it for the actual claimant or lead. This follows Electric's warning about losing write context and the project's rule never to silently discard unsynced work.
- Use HTTP 200 with a typed rejection body (or the platform's equivalent) for business rejections, so the client's upload queue does not stall, as PowerSync advises. Reserve 5xx for transient failures that should be retried.
- Revocation while offline: a user removed from a team while offline can still see the cached queue and attempt claims. The server rejects those claims, and the next sync should purge local rows outside the user's scope. This is the "last-known authorization" principle.

### Gaps
- I found no primary Linear documentation on how rejected transactions surface to users.
- I did not verify Replicache's older push/pull docs separately. The Zero docs are the current Rocicorp source.

## 4. Permission models: roles versus teams, record scope, backend enforcement

### Takeaway
Keep two orthogonal axes. A **role** (or privilege set) says which actions a member may perform in a business and module. **Team membership** says where work routes and which records are in scope. Relationship-based models (Zanzibar, OpenFGA, SpiceDB) express "member of team T can see T's unassigned tasks" directly as relations. OWASP recommends ABAC/ReBAC over pure RBAC for applications, denying by default and checking objects on every request. In Postgres, row-level security can enforce "my assigned items plus my teams' unassigned items" at the database, with known caveats. Sync engines must apply the same scope to what they replicate to devices.

### Cited Findings
- **OWASP.** "Deny access by default"; least privilege; "permission should be validated correctly on every request"; "ABAC and ReBAC should typically be preferred for application development", because RBAC handles context poorly, role checks are easy to miss, roles multiply ("role explosion"), and RBAC fits multi-tenant setups poorly. Run access checks "for the specific object or functionality being accessed" to prevent IDOR. "Developers must never rely on client-side access control checks." — [OWASP Authorization Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authorization_Cheat_Sheet.html)
- **Zanzibar.** Google's authorization system, presented at USENIX ATC 2019, scales "to trillions of access control lists and millions of authorization requests per second". Decisions respect the causal order of user actions (external consistency). — [Google Research, Zanzibar](https://research.google/pubs/zanzibar-googles-consistent-global-authorization-system/)
- **The new enemy problem.** If permission checks use stale ACLs, revoked users may see new content, or old ACLs get applied to new content. Zanzibar's fix is "zookies": a permission snapshot token stored with content, guaranteeing checks are "at least as fresh" as that token. The trade-off is a deliberate bias toward false negatives, so recently granted access may briefly be denied. — [Authzed, "New enemies"](https://authzed.com/blog/new-enemies)
- **OpenFGA teams.** A tuple makes `user:alice` a `member` of `team:writers`, and a userset such as `team:writers#member` can then be granted a relation on an object. OpenFGA expands usersets "into all its individual user identifiers". Nested teams can inherit parent-team access. — [OpenFGA user groups](https://openfga.dev/docs/modeling/user-groups); [OpenFGA GitHub model](https://openfga.dev/docs/modeling/advanced/github); [OpenFGA concepts](https://openfga.dev/docs/concepts.md)
- **Postgres RLS.** With RLS enabled and no policy, "a default-deny policy is used". `USING` filters which existing rows are visible or targetable, and rows failing it are silently skipped. `WITH CHECK` validates new or updated rows and raises an error on violation. Permissive policies combine with OR, restrictive ones with AND. Superusers, `BYPASSRLS` roles and normally table owners bypass RLS unless `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` is set. Policies whose subqueries read other tables (for example a membership table) can race with concurrent membership changes under READ COMMITTED. Mitigations include `FOR SHARE` in the subquery or locking the referenced table on change. — [PostgreSQL row security policies](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/ddl-rowsecurity.html)

### Inferences
- **Suggested data model, relational and without a generic engine:**
  - `membership(business_id, user_id, role)`, where the role maps to an action privilege set, for example `tasks.claim`, `tasks.assign_others`, `tasks.view_all`.
  - `team_membership(team_id, user_id, is_lead)`, many-to-many, so one person can be on several teams.
  - `task(business_id, team_id NULL, assignee_id NULL, created_by, ...)`.
- **Visibility predicate** (enforce it in the API query layer and, defence in depth, in RLS with `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` for the Suite runtime role): `assignee_id = me OR created_by = me OR (team_id IN my_teams AND (assignee_id IS NULL OR my_role_allows('tasks.view_team') OR is_lead_of(team_id))) OR my_role_allows('tasks.view_all')`.
- **Action predicates** (each checked server-side on every command):
  - claim: has the `tasks.claim` privilege, `team_id ∈ my_teams` and `assignee_id IS NULL`.
  - unclaim: I am the assignee, or a lead of the team.
  - reassign or assign to someone else: privilege plus lead-of-team or manager role, and the target is a member of the team.
  - complete: I am the assignee.
- The lead relationship is a team relation (ReBAC-style), not a global role. Being a lead of team A grants nothing on team B. This avoids role explosion such as a separate "kitchen-lead" or "sales-lead" role, which is OWASP's concern.
- Membership changes should take effect for routing and visibility at the next server check. Removing someone from a team should not automatically unassign their claimed tasks. It should flag those tasks for the lead, because silent reassignment breaks accountability. Whether access to tasks you still hold continues is a product decision; record it.
- Sync scope (what gets replicated to a device) must use the same predicate as API reads. Otherwise the local database leaks records the API would deny. OWASP's "never rely on client-side checks" applies doubly offline, per the Ink & Switch remark about local copies.
- Zookie-style consistency is overkill for a small business. The lightweight analogue: every command is checked against current membership in the same transaction as the write, so the new enemy problem cannot arise for writes. Reads may be briefly stale on devices, which is acceptable only because sync then purges out-of-scope rows.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch SpiceDB schema docs. OpenFGA covers the same Zanzibar-style concepts.
- I found no source quantifying the performance of RLS membership subqueries at small scale.

## 5. Fan-out versus shared task

### Takeaway
"Someone on the team should do X" is one task routed to a team queue with a single eventual owner. "Everyone on the team must do X" is a parent item that fans out into one child record per member: a child task, or a lighter acknowledgement record. Each child has its own single owner and status, and the parent's completion is derived from its children. Asana's own guidance, one assignee on the parent with assigned subtasks or assigned copies, is the primary product precedent.

### Cited Findings
- Asana recommends one assignee on a parent accountable for the deadline, with subtasks assigned to individuals. It also offers "assign copies" of a task to several people. — [Asana, "Why one assignee?"](https://asana.com/resources/why-one-assignee); [Asana, assign copies](https://asana.com/id/inside-asana/assign-copies-of-a-task.md)
- Diffusion of responsibility is strongest when a request is addressed to a group, so "everyone must" requirements addressed to a group without per-person records are prone to non-response. — [Barron and Yechiam 2002 record](https://datalearner.com:8443/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/72/paper-detail/71520)

### Inferences
- **Shared ("anyone") task.** A single row with `team_id` set and `assignee_id` null until claimed. Completion by the claimant completes it. Use this for "call the supplier back" or "prep the order".
- **Fan-out ("everyone") task.** A parent row plus one child per member, materialised at creation. Each child is created with `assignee_id` set, so there is no claim race. The parent shows "4 of 6 done" and has its own accountable owner, typically the creator or the lead, who answers for the whole.
- **Snapshot versus live membership.** Record which policy applies to fan-out. Either children are created for the team at creation time (snapshot, auditable), or members who join later get children added (live). Snapshot is simpler and fits the "never silently change records" principle. Members who leave keep their children, flagged for the lead.
- **Acknowledgement-only items**, such as "read the new allergen policy", can use a lighter `acknowledgement(item_id, user_id, acknowledged_at)` record instead of full child tasks. Offline acknowledgements are idempotent and conflict-free, because each user writes only their own row, so they can be accepted with no claim semantics.
- Offline, fan-out has none of the claim-conflict problems, since each child has a fixed owner. Its main offline risk is the parent's creator seeing stale progress.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical research comparing fan-out against shared tasks for completion rates. The recommendation rests on product precedent and the diffusion-of-responsibility literature.
